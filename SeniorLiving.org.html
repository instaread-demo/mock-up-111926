Do I Need a VPN? A Clear Answer for Seniors

A VPN can add privacy to your connection. Here’s how we’d decide whether it belongs in your daily routine and your budget.

A VPN can be useful, but you don’t need one just because you go online. We’d consider it if you regularly use shared Wi-Fi or want more privacy from the company providing your internet connection. If you mostly browse at home, there are other security habits we’d check before adding another subscription to your retirement budget.

When we evaluate VPNs, we look beyond the list of features. Can you tell when the connection is protected? Does the app work comfortably on the devices you use? And will the service still be worth its renewal price? Those questions matter more than an advertisement telling you everyone needs a VPN.

Below, we’ll help you decide when a VPN is useful, explain its limits, and walk through what we’d do before paying for one.

The Short Answer: It Depends on the Privacy You Want

We’d treat a VPN as an optional privacy tool, rather than a requirement for banking, shopping, or using the internet safely. Here’s where we’d start:

Your situation Our recommendation
You often connect to Wi-Fi at hotels, airports, libraries, or other shared locations. Consider a VPN for an additional layer of connection privacy.
You’d prefer to limit what your internet provider can learn about the websites you visit. A reputable VPN can help, although you’ll be trusting the VPN provider with your connection instead.
You mostly use an updated device on a well-secured home network. A VPN is optional. We’d check passwords, account security, and updates first.
You want to stop scam calls, fake emails, or fraudulent payment requests. A VPN won’t solve that problem. Focus on scam prevention and account security.

When We’d Consider Adding a VPN

You Regularly Use Shared Wi-Fi

If you take a tablet to the library or connect your laptop at a hotel, a VPN can encrypt traffic between that device and the VPN server. That gives you an additional layer of privacy on a network someone else manages.

There’s an important qualification: Most websites already encrypt information using HTTPS. The FTC explains that this widespread encryption makes public Wi-Fi generally safe to use. Without a VPN, someone sharing the network can’t simply read the contents of a properly encrypted banking session.

We’d still confirm the network name with the business, pay attention to browser security warnings, and avoid entering personal information on an unfamiliar site. A VPN doesn’t make a fake website trustworthy.

You Want More Privacy From Your Internet Provider

The company providing your current internet connection carries your traffic. That could be your home internet provider, your mobile carrier, or the provider serving a hotel. A properly configured VPN can limit that provider’s visibility into your browsing destinations for traffic routed through the VPN’s encrypted connection.

That doesn’t remove the need for trust—it changes whom you’re trusting. Your internet provider can generally see that you’re connected to a VPN, while the VPN provider handles the onward connection. We’d look for clear privacy policies and independent assessments of the provider’s no-logs claims.

You Spend Time Away From Home

For someone who travels often, we like the convenience of using the same VPN app across different networks. Automatic connection settings can help make that routine easier, although you’ll need to enable and check them.

We wouldn’t buy a VPN solely on a promise that it will always unlock a particular streaming service overseas. Streaming access can change, and a different IP address can also trigger extra sign-in checks from a bank or another account provider.

When We’d Put a VPN Lower on the List

If you mainly use a well-secured home network and updated devices, a VPN may not be your next priority. We’d first check that your router uses modern Wi-Fi security, its software is updated, and it has a unique administrative password (not the factory default password).

Banking or shopping from home doesn’t automatically change that answer. HTTPS protects the information traveling between your browser and a legitimate website. A VPN can add connection privacy, but it doesn’t replace that protection or determine whether the business is honest.

The same applies to an iPhone or another smartphone. Using cellular data rather than shared Wi-Fi changes the network you’re trusting, but it doesn’t make a VPN compulsory or stop a fraudulent message from reaching you.

What a VPN Changes—and What It Doesn’t

We find it helpful to separate connection privacy from the other things people mean by “online safety.”

What you want to protect What a VPN can do What it won’t do
Traffic between your device and the VPN server Encrypt traffic routed through the VPN tunnel Guarantee that your device, destination website, or every app is secure
Your household’s public IP address Let websites see the VPN server’s IP address instead Hide information you provide, accounts you sign into, or location access you allow
Browsing privacy on the local network Reduce what the network operator can see about traffic inside the tunnel Make the VPN provider irrelevant to your privacy
Protection from scams or malware Some VPN subscriptions include separate blocking or security tools Make the VPN connection itself a replacement for scam awareness or device security

It Won’t Stop All Advertiser Tracking

An IP address is only one way companies recognize visitors. If you sign into an account, accept advertising cookies, or give an app permission to use your location, a VPN won’t undo those choices.

If unwanted tracking is your main concern, we’d review browser privacy settings, cookie choices, and app permissions, too. The FTC recommends reviewing those controls to limit the information websites and apps collect.2 Buying a VPN and leaving every other privacy setting unchanged won’t address the whole problem.

It Won’t Recognize Every Scam for You

Suppose a message claims your account is locked and asks you to open a link. Connecting a VPN won’t tell you whether that message is genuine. We’d open the company’s app directly or use a phone number from a statement or their website instead.

The same goes for someone asking you to send money, share a verification code, or give them remote access to your device. A VPN can’t decide whether you should trust that person.

Did You Know?

Did You Know? Americans over age 60 reported approximately $7.7 billion in internet-crime losses in 2025.3 That figure covers crimes a VPN may do nothing to prevent.

A Self-Check Without a Score to Reach

We wouldn’t tell a loved one to buy a VPN simply because they answered “yes” to a certain number of questions. Instead, we’d work through these three:

  1. What are we trying to improve? More privacy from a shared network or internet provider is a reasonable VPN goal. Stopping scam calls or securing an already compromised account calls for a different response.
  2. Where will we use it? List the devices and networks involved. Someone who travels with a laptop has different needs from someone who uses one phone at home.
  3. Will the cost and upkeep be manageable? Consider the upfront payment, renewal terms, and whether you’re comfortable checking the app’s connection status.

If you can name a privacy benefit you want and can comfortably manage the service, a VPN is worth considering. If the problem you want to solve falls outside its scope, we’d address that problem first.

The Security Habits We’d Check Before Adding Another App

These steps matter whether you decide to use a VPN or skip one:

  • Use a different, strong password for each important account. A password manager can help you create and keep track of them.
  • Turn on multifactor authentication where available. It requires more than one type of proof that it’s you, such as a password and a code from an authenticator app.4 Start with your primary email and financial accounts.
  • Keep devices and apps updated. Enable automatic updates where practical, and keep the device’s security protections switched on.
  • Check where a message is sending you. We’d use a saved bookmark or open the official app for a sensitive account, rather than follow an unexpected sign-in link.
  • Use authorized access when helping someone else. If you’re a caregiver, ask whether the service offers caregiver or proxy access. A VPN doesn’t create permission to use another person’s account.

Could a Free VPN Be Enough?

It could. We’d judge a free plan by its provider, privacy practices, and limitations, rather than assume all free VPNs are alike.

For example, Proton VPN Free offers unlimited data with one connection at a time. It has fewer server choices and features than its paid service, but it may be sufficient for basic connection privacy on one device. There’s no reason to upgrade if the free plan already meets your needs.

Before You Pay, Check the Terms

A low advertised monthly price may require payment for several years upfront. We’d check the full charge, the number of months included, and what the next payment buys before deciding whether the offer is affordable.

It’s always best to read subscription details and find out how to cancel before signing up.5 Save the order confirmation and note both the refund deadline and renewal date.

Keep these three offers separate:

  • An ongoing free plan lets you use the service without buying a subscription, subject to its limits.
  • A free trial provides access for a limited period and may require payment details or convert to a paid subscription.
  • A money-back guarantee requires payment first, followed by an eligible refund request within the stated window.

NordVPN’s 30-day money-back guarantee applies to eligible initial purchases—it isn’t a universal 30-day free trial. Check the rules for your purchase channel. Turning off automatic renewal and requesting a refund are separate actions, so don’t assume one completes the other.

Getting Started With a VPN

If you decide to go ahead, we’d keep the first setup simple:

  1. Choose the plan and save its terms. Confirm the charge, billing period, renewal details, and refund policy.
  2. Install the official app. Use the provider’s website or its verified listing in your device’s app store.
  3. Connect and check the status. Start with the recommended or automatic connection. Don’t assume installing the app means the VPN is active.
  4. Try your usual activities. Open familiar websites, make a video call, and try any streaming service you use. Do this while you can still request a refund under the applicable terms.
  5. Make the routine your own. If someone helps with setup, practice connecting and disconnecting while they’re still available. Set reminders for the refund deadline and renewal date.

If a website stops working, don’t ignore a security warning or enter your information on an unfamiliar replacement site. Check the provider’s troubleshooting guidance or contact support.

The Bottom Line

We’d recommend a VPN when its connection privacy solves a problem you actually have. Regular shared Wi-Fi use or a desire to limit your internet provider’s visibility can make it a useful addition. Banking online, owning a smartphone, or helping a loved one with technology doesn’t make it mandatory.

If you decide it fits, NordVPN is a sensible place to start comparing. If you decide to wait, keep up with passwords, account security, updates, and scam awareness. Those habits are important either way.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Do You Need a VPN for Online Banking?

    Not automatically. A legitimate bank’s HTTPS website or properly secured app already encrypts its communications. A VPN can add connection privacy, but you should still verify that you’re using the real bank’s website or app and enable its account-security options.

  • Is a VPN Necessary on a Phone?

    It’s optional. We’d base the decision on the networks you use and the privacy you want, rather than whether your phone is an iPhone or Android. Cellular service doesn’t remove the possibility of phishing, account theft, or other scams.

  • Will a VPN Slow Down Our Internet?

    It can. The effect varies with the provider, server distance, internet connection, device, and settings. The difference in speed is typically small, but all of these factors can have a cumulative effect. We’d start with a nearby recommended server and try the activities that matter to you before the applicable refund deadline.

  • Does Private Browsing Replace a VPN?

    No. A private or incognito browser window mainly limits what that browser saves locally after the session. It doesn’t provide the same network routing as a VPN, and neither tool makes you completely anonymous online.

  • Can We Cancel a VPN by Deleting the App?

    No. Deleting the app generally doesn’t cancel billing. Follow the cancellation process for the provider or app store where you subscribed, and save the confirmation. If you also want a refund, check whether you need to request that separately.
