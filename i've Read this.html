Book Review: Restaurant Kid by Rachel Phan

ByAnne Logan November 16, 2026
Book Reviews | Memoir | Non-fiction

Immigration is a hot topic in Canada these days, especially here in Alberta where our most recent referendum polled people on the idea of restricting access to certain public programs for those new to Canada. Many governments benefit from differentiating those who are born ‘here’ vs. ‘elsewhere’, but luckily we have memoirs like Restaurant Kid by Rachel Phan to keep reminding us of the similarities we all share. Restaurant Kid is written from the perspective of a young woman who was only one of two racialized people in her entire school, so her unique lens is one many will find eye-opening. The struggles she faces aren’t just with relating to those in her small-town of Ontario, but also her family members themselves. The subtitle of the book is “A Memoir of Family and Belonging”, which is a clue as to where the growth in Phan’s views are rooted.

Book Summary

Rachel Phan’s parents fled both China and the Vietnam War to come to Canada and start a new life, seeking stability and opportunity. They situated in a small town with less than a 5% racialized population, and they opened a Chinese food restaurant serving ‘western style’ Chinese food. The youngest of three children Rachel was spared her family’s expectations of working in the family business, given the freedom to socialize with friends and pursue her own interests while her older brother and sister spent most of their time with their parents, tending bar, taking orders, doing dishes, and anything else that was needed to keep the busy restaurant afloat. Rachel’s parents had very little time for anything but work, so she found herself adrift, desperate for attention. She struggles with the resentment of having absent parents, but also feels guilty for the luxuries she enjoys compared to the poverty-stricken lives her mother and father led when they were her age. Because her family lives in such a rural area, Rachel also struggles with the casual racism she faces on a daily basis, ranging from insulting questions to her appearance, to shouted slurs from passing cars. Phan’s memoir is a coming-of-age story that dives deep into the challenges of relating to neither one’s family, nor the place you call home. It isn’t until she becomes an adult herself and travels back to Vietnam with her parents that she begins to feel a sense of belonging she had been missing all along.

My Thoughts

The author is still a young woman, so much of this book focuses on her childhood and teenage years. As Phan grows up and learns more about her parents, the readers grow along with her, turning from a stubborn, resentful child who willfully breaks plates, to a teenage girl searching for approval in the young men she dates, to the young adult who finally begins to see her parents as not just “Mom and Dad”, but individuals themselves. This trajectory prevents the book from turning into a litany of complaints that readers would quickly tire from. Instead, our perspective is shaped alongside hers, and I was surprised at how vastly different my opinions of her parents were from the beginning of the book to the end. And it’s not just realizations about her family; Phan is caught between two very conflicting cultures:

“I later learned the term ‘third-culture kid,’ which is used to describe a child who grows up in a culture different from the one their parents grew up in…I’m somehow both ‘too Chinese’ and ‘too Canadian’. No matter where I am, I am too much of something to truly fit in…I became whoever the person I was talking to expected me to be” (p. 60).

Another aspect of this book that I found fascinating was the glimpse into the life of owning a family restaurant, which not surprisingly, is all consuming. I think most would agree the hospitality industry is never easy, but when your family is dependent on on this one business for its entire livelihood, it becomes the center of the universe. There are certain things that sound appealing for kids (never-ending food, a paid job you can slip in and out of) but it’s quickly apparent how draining it can be when the customers and their wants always come before your own.

Growing up is hard for everyone, but Phan’s unique story is one I both enjoyed and appreciated learning more about. Although you won’t find anything shocking in this memoir, it was a surprise to me how much her emotions mirrored my own as I grew up, albeit in very difference circumstances. Yet another reason to read widely and enjoy the experience of living in another person’s shoes, if only for a few hundred pages or so!
