Adapted from Why Sustainable Seafood Matters: Making Environmentally Conscious Choices (Johns Hopkins University Press)

People used to think that the oceans were so vast that we could take anything that we wanted out of them, and we’d never have an impact. That all changed after Canadian cod were overfished.

The presence of so many fish played a larger-than-commonly-appreciated role in European desires to colonize North America. If you’ve never heard this before, I recommend reading Mark Kurlansky’s aptly titled Cod: A Biography of the Fish That Changed the World. Kurlansky writes:

“…in 1497, five years after Columbus first stumbled across the Caribbean, Giovanni Cabot sailed from Bristol…Cabot found land, though it wasn’t Asia. It was a vast, rocky coastline that was ideal for salting and drying fish, by a sea that was teeming with cod. Cabot reported on the cod as evidence of the wealth on the new land.”

There were so many cod in the water that early Newfoundlanders didn’t have to use bait, they simply lowered baskets into the ocean on a rope, waited a few seconds, and hauled in baskets full of cod!

As fishing technology increased, so did catch, for many years. From the 1850s to the 1950s, cod catch steadily increased from about 100,000 tons a year to about 300,000 tons a year.

Everything changed with the invention of a revolutionary new technology. Can you guess what it is? It’s not a new kind of fishing gear, and it’s not a new kind of boat engine. I bet you have it in your home. Give up?

That’s right, the revolutionary new technology is refrigeration. This allowed larger boats to stay at sea longer, keeping fish fresh without having to go to shore to offload their catch before it spoiled. As Kurlansky put it, “[Frozen vegetable company] Birdseye undeniably changed our civilization.” Annual catch soon doubled to over 600,000 tons a year, and climbed higher still, reaching a peak of just over 800,000 tons in the 1970s. Soon after, no matter how hard we fished, there just weren’t enough cod. By the 1980s, catch was down to pre-World War II levels despite many more (and much larger) boats on the water fishing for longer, and in 1992 the Canadian government took the once-unthinkable step of closing the cod fishery. Cod populations still haven’t recovered, the few that were left weren’t able to have enough babies to replenish once-vast stocks.


By Lamiot – Own work, CC BY-SA 3.0, Link

As you might imagine, this isn’t just bad for cod, but for the entire marine ecosystem — losing that much biomass has a major disruption, the full effects of which we still don’t fully understand.

And it’s also bad for the human beings who depended on this once-abundant natural resource. The socioeconomic impact of the cod fishery collapse on Atlantic Canada can still be felt today, decades later. Historic fishing communities that were once among the world’s most prosperous are struggling. Some have pivoted to tourism, taking advantage of Atlantic Canada’s beautiful coastline — I personally loved my visits to Lunenburg, Nova Scotia, and St. Johns, Newfoundland! But many once-vibrant once-thriving communities are gone or are full of people on welfare who still feel left behind and bitter. Cod still haven’t recovered, but some former fishermen earnestly believe that they only reason they aren’t catching cod is because the evil government said they’re not allowed.

The cultural aspect of fishing is important, and it’s why I open my lectures on overfishing with a musical interlude, playing “Fishermen’s Lament” by the Canadian folk band Great Big Sea. I encourage you to listen to the haunting melody yourself:

Can you imagine a song with that kind of emotion behind it being written about, say, the 2023 bankruptcy and closing of Bed, Bath, and Beyond? I certainly can’t.

This is one of the reasons why discussions of what to do about fisheries management get so heated. Some jobs are more than what you do for income, they’re a calling, they’re who you are, and the presence of those jobs in the community is who we are as a people. A similar dynamic occurs in U.S. politics with respect to coal miners and their outsized role in discussions of transitions to renewable energy — in the 2016 presidential election, it was pointed out that many more people work for the UPMC hospital system in my hometown of Pittsburgh (around 100,000 jobs) than work for all the coal mines in the whole state of West Virginia (around 10,000 jobs), but you’d never know that from the tone and relative focus of some of the media coverage and political speeches.

But back to overfishing. You may be wondering why no one noticed that something was wrong before it was too late. Part of the answer is that nothing like this had ever happened before, at least not on this scale, to a natural resource that was so important and so well known. Many people truly did not understand that it was even possible to collapse a resource that had supported communities for centuries.

But also, some people absolutely did notice and absolutely tried to sound the alarm. One, Ransom A (RAM) Myers, was a Canadian scientist who tried to get the government reduce fishing pressure while there were still enough cod left to recover the stocks. He was ridiculed, threatened, and ultimately ignored. But he followed the data and never backed down, notably titling one of his PowerPoint presentations “I am right and here’s why.” One of my professional societies named a conference plenary address after him and his courageous, though ultimately unsuccessful, calls to action.
