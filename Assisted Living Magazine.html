Longevity Technology Needs to Do More Than Measure Health

As companies develop new ways to monitor and manage our health, an important question remains: Will these inventions reach the people who may need them most?

Imagine two people monitoring their health. The first is 35, wears a smartwatch, tracks sleep and exercise, and regularly reviews blood test results through an app. The second is 85, takes several medications, sees multiple specialists, and relies on a daughter to help coordinate appointments and follow-up care.

Both could benefit enormously from advances in healthcare technology. Their needs, however, may differ greatly. A tool that helps someone track daily wellness may also need to support medication changes, caregiver involvement, and coordination across several clinicians.

A report from venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz, The State of Longevity, explores consumer interest in wearable technology, artificial intelligence, advanced testing, and customized healthcare. Drawing on a survey of 500 U.S. consumers, it also argues that an important opportunity lies in helping people act on health information and obtain follow-up care.

That is a hopeful path. For older adults with complicated healthcare needs, the question is how well these systems will fit into everyday care. In assisted living communities, where residents, families, staff, and outside clinicians may all be involved, useful technology needs to make their shared work easier.

More Health Data Doesn’t Necessarily Mean Better Care

One of the more revealing findings in the a16z survey concerns wearable technology. Among respondents, 54% used a wearable device, and 83% of those users checked it daily. Yet 55% of wearable users said their data had never prompted a change in behavior or a doctor’s visit.

That does not necessarily mean the device was unhelpful. It does, however, raise a useful question about what happens after information is collected. A small consumer survey cannot establish whether the entire longevity industry is meeting the needs of older adults.

A smartwatch may detect an irregular heart rhythm. A home blood pressure monitor may reveal elevated readings. The value of that information depends on whether someone can interpret it, determine whether it matters, and respond appropriately.

For older adults managing multiple conditions, this process can be particularly complicated. An abnormal reading may need to be considered alongside existing diagnoses and medications, with follow-up involving several healthcare professionals. Better measurement needs to be accompanied by a clear path to appropriate care.

Older Adults Are Adopting Technology

It would be a mistake to assume that older adults are uninterested in digital health.

AARP’s summary of its 2026 technology trends report found that 90% of adults age 50 and older owned a smartphone, while 36% owned a wearable device. Yet 60% believed technology was not designed with their age group in mind. These findings describe adults 50 and older, a broad population that should not be treated as interchangeable with people in their 80s who have complex care needs.

Older adults are not a uniform population. Some are highly comfortable with technology, while others experience changes in vision, hearing, memory, or dexterity that make certain devices difficult to use. Some prefer to manage their health independently, while others rely on family members or caregivers.

The challenge is to develop technologies that reflect the circumstances in which people actually live. For more on how physical and cognitive abilities shape support needs, see our article on intrinsic capacity in older adults.

The Hidden Work of Managing Healthcare

For many older adults and their families, one of the greatest challenges consists of navigating what happens after a medical visit or hospital stay. Consider someone returning home after a hospitalization. They may leave with a revised medication list, rehabilitation instructions, appointments with several specialists, and recommendations for monitoring symptoms. Each step requires coordination, much of it undertaken by family members.

AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving’s July 2026 report, drawing on the Caregiving in the US 2025 survey, estimated that more than 51 million Americans cared for adults age 50 and older. These family caregivers provided an average of about 26 hours of care per week.

Technology is already part of that work for some families. A NORC survey conducted in December 2025 and published in February 2026 found that 48% of unpaid caregivers used at least one technology to support caregiving. That finding covers care recipients of all ages, including children, rather than only older adults. It measures technology use, not whether the tools improved care.

Digital platforms could help families organize appointments, share updated care information with authorized people, and keep track of questions for clinicians. AI might assist clinicians in reviewing complex information, but medication reconciliation and treatment decisions need appropriate professional oversight.

For assisted living residents, the practical test is whether a tool helps families, staff, and outside clinicians understand what needs to happen next and who is responsible. A new alert has limited value if no one knows who should review it or how to arrange follow-up.

Building for the People Who Need Care

There is good reason for excitement about advances in longevity science and healthcare technology. Wearable devices may make health monitoring more convenient, and digital tools may help clinicians and families make sense of information. Their benefits will depend on who can use them, how they fit into existing healthcare systems, and whether they meaningfully improve care.

A useful system should help an older adult get the right medication after leaving the hospital, give a family caregiver clear instructions about when to seek help, and make relevant information available to a clinician. Caregiver involvement should reflect the older adult’s consent and preferences, with appropriate access controls. Medication questions should reach a qualified professional rather than being resolved independently by AI.

These are practical problems that older adults and families encounter every day. Greater insight into health is worthwhile, but acting on it also requires access to care and support. The measure of progress should be whether that knowledge makes healthcare easier to navigate and more responsive to the people who need it most.
